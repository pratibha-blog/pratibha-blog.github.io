A Believer of Equality

A father's daughter or a mother's son,
Since when did we start compromising on all the fun?
The fun life throws on,
The way life goes on.

Warriors were the daughters,
Chefs were the sons,
It was always equal,
Whether they lost or won.

A win or a loss was always gender-neutral,
Then why do we discriminate?
Why must a dream have a gender,
And a destiny come with a fate?

Why should she be taught to be softer,
While he is taught to be strong?
Why should she be told to stay behind,
While he is told to move along?

Let children be children,
Let dreams be dreams,
Let courage have no gender,
And life be more than it seems.

Let her build, let him nurture,
Let her lead, let him care,
Let them choose their own colours,
Their own paths, their own share.

For life was never meant to be
A list of things "she" or "he" can do.
It was meant to be lived freely
By me, by you, by all of us too.

So, a father's daughter or a mother's son,
Let's stop deciding where they belong.
Give them the freedom to become
Who they were meant to be all along.

What Ganesh Chaturthi taught me about giving back

Every year around this time, the same ritual plays out in homes across the country: a clay idol arrives, sits with us for a few days, and then returns to the water it came from. As a kid I thought this was just tradition. Now I think it might be one of the most quietly profound ideas we grow up with.

The idol is made of earth. It goes back to the earth. Nothing is kept, nothing is wasted, nothing is permanent and somehow that's the whole point. We welcome something sacred into our homes, and then we let it go, back into the river, the soil, the cycle it belongs to.

This year I found myself thinking less about the festival and more about that cycle itself. How much we take from the earth without noticing, and how rarely we pause to give anything back. Ganesh Chaturthi, at its quiet core, is a lesson in exactly that borrow gently, return gracefully, stay grateful the whole way through.

I don't think you need to be religious to feel that. You just need to have stood by a river once, or planted something, or watched clay dry in the sun. Mother Nature doesn't ask for grand gestures. She just asks that we notice.

So this year, that's my small takeaway not just celebrating a festival, but remembering what it's quietly been trying to teach me all along.
